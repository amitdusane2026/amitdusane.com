Why it matters to you

A published fix is not the same as a fix every visitor has. Between the server and the visitor there are saved copies of the old files. Knowing where they are explains the hours between "it is live" and "it works everywhere", and it stops you testing the old version by mistake.

Why sites keep copies

Downloading every file on every visit would make sites slow. So browsers and networks keep saved copies of files, called a cache. A visitor who comes back gets the saved copy, and the page appears faster.

Think of a supermarket. The factory changes a recipe, but the shelves are still full of boxes made last week. Customers buy the old recipe until those boxes are sold. Nobody did anything wrong. The new recipe simply has to wait for the old stock to run out.

Three places an old copy can live

Browser cacheOn the visitor's own device. Kept until it expires, or until the visitor forces a reload.
CDNOn servers around the world between the site and its visitors. Kept until it expires, or until the team clears it.
Server cacheOn the site's own servers, for pages that take work to build. Kept until the site rebuilds them.

A CDN, or content delivery network, keeps copies of a site's files in many countries so they reach visitors quickly. Akamai, Cloudflare and Fastly are the names you will hear most. Adobe Tags libraries are also delivered from a CDN.

How long a copy is kept

Every file comes with an instruction that says how long a saved copy may be used. That period is its time to live, often shortened to TTL. A long TTL makes the site fast and changes slow. A short TTL makes changes fast and the site a little slower.

Scripts are often kept for hours or days. So a change to a script can take that long to reach everyone, even after it is live on the server.

The real fix is a new name

The reliable way around old copies is cache busting: giving a file a new name, or a new code in its address, whenever its content changes. A copy saved under the old name can never be mistaken for the new file.

Most modern build tools do this automatically, with names such as app.4f3a9c.js. When a change seems to reach some visitors and not others, ask whether the file name changed with it.

A team can also purge the CDN, which deletes the saved copies there so every visitor receives the new file. A purge does not reach the copies already saved inside visitors' browsers.

Checking which version you are looking at

Your own browser keeps saved copies too. Before you test a fix, do a hard refresh: Ctrl+Shift+R on Windows, or Cmd+Shift+R on a Mac in Chrome. In the network tab you can also tick Disable cache, which ignores saved copies while the tools are open.

On a site with Adobe Tags, you can check the library directly. Type this into the console and compare the build time with the time you published.

Where this goes wrong

A fix is published and tested at once. The tester's browser still holds yesterday's library, so the test fails. A second fix is published on top of the first, which was working. Now there are two changes to separate instead of one.

So after any publish, confirm the version first and test second. It adds a minute, and it avoids the most confusing kind of failed test: one where the fix was right and the test was looking at the past.
