Why it matters to you

Every page view in your reports says two things: a page loaded, and your tracking ran on it. Most arguments about missing or doubled page views are really about the order in which a page is built. When you know that order, you can say where a number went wrong instead of guessing.

A page is built from many requests

Typing an address and pressing Enter makes the browser send a request to a server, the computer that holds the website.

The server sends back a response. That first response is not the finished page. It is one text file of HTML, a list of instructions for building the page.

The browser reads that file from the top. Every time it finds a picture, a style file or a script, it sends another request. A normal page sends dozens of them, and a busy one sends more than a hundred. Your tracking call is one of those requests, and it usually leaves near the end.

So a page view is not recorded when a visitor arrives. It is recorded when the page has been built far enough for your tracking to run. Everything that happens before that moment is invisible to your reports.

Think of a restaurant bill

A page load works like a meal in a restaurant. You order, and the dishes arrive one by one: bread, then soup, then the main course. Each dish takes its own time, and some arrive together.

Your tracking call is the bill. The restaurant only records the meal when the bill is printed. A guest who leaves after the soup is never recorded. A bill printed twice records two meals. In both cases the kitchen did its job. The record is what went wrong.

Status codes say what the server answered

Every response carries a status code, a three-digit number. You will see these numbers in the browser's network tab, and developers use them all the time.

200Success. The server sent what was asked for.
301, 302Moved. The server gives a new address, and the browser goes there.
304Not changed. The browser uses the copy it already saved.
404Not found. Nothing exists at that address.
500The server failed while answering.

A 404 page is still a page, so it can still load your tracking. That is how a report can show views of an address that does not exist. The row does not prove the page is real. It only proves that somebody asked for that address.

Redirects can lose your campaign codes

A redirect is a response that says the page has moved and gives the new address. The browser follows it at once, and the visitor does not notice. Redirects are normal. An old product page points to a new one, and every http address points to its https version.

The risk is the part of the address after the question mark, where campaign codes are kept. A redirect that does not copy that part onto the new address throws the campaign code away. The visit still arrives, but your reports count it as direct traffic.

Where this goes wrong

An email links to an old address. The server redirects it to the new page and drops everything after the question mark. For weeks the email seems to bring no visits, and direct traffic seems to grow. Nothing looks broken to the visitor, so nobody reports a problem.

When the tracking runs decides who is counted

Two moments matter. The first is when the browser has read the HTML and the page structure exists. The second is the page load event, when the pictures and scripts have finished too. Your page view can be set to fire at either one.

Firing early counts more visitors, including people who leave in the first second. Firing late misses those people, but more values are ready by then, such as details the page adds as it loads.

Neither choice is wrong. The mistake is changing from one to the other without telling anyone, because every trend line moves on that day.

How Adobe Analytics records that moment is covered in Page View Tracking.

What a faster page can do to your numbers

People leave slow pages. If the tracking runs near the end, the people who leave first are never recorded. Your bounce rate then looks better than the truth, because the visitors most likely to bounce were never counted.

So when a developer says a change will make a page faster, ask one more question: does it make the tracking run earlier or later? A faster page with later tracking can show fewer visits in your reports even while more people see it.

To watch these requests yourself, open the browser's developer tools.
