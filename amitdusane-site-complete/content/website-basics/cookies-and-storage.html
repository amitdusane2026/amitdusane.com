Why it matters to you

A unique visitor in your reports is really a unique cookie. Clear the cookie and the same person becomes a new visitor. Every major browser now limits cookies, so your visitor numbers depend partly on decisions made by Apple, Mozilla and Google.

A cookie is a cloakroom ticket

When you leave a coat in a cloakroom, you get a ticket with a number. The ticket does not describe you. It only lets the cloakroom match you to the same coat later.

A cookie works the same way. On the first visit, the site gives the browser a random ID and asks it to keep it. On the next visit, the browser shows the ID again, and the visit is joined to the earlier ones. Lose the ticket, and the cloakroom sees a new customer.

The ticket belongs to a browser, not a person. Somebody who uses a phone, a laptop and a work computer holds three tickets, and appears as three visitors until they sign in somewhere their visits can be joined.

First-party and third-party cookies

Every cookie belongs to a domain. A first-party cookie belongs to the site in the address bar. A third-party cookie belongs to another company's domain, such as an advertising network, and is set while the visitor is on your site.

Safari and Firefox block third-party cookies by default, and Chrome lets people block them. So analytics relies on first-party cookies. Adobe keeps its visitor ID in a first-party cookie on your own domain.

What that ID is and how it joins visits is covered in Experience Cloud ID (ECID).

The browser decides how long a cookie lasts

A site asks for an expiry date when it sets a cookie. The browser does not always agree. Safari limits cookies written by JavaScript to seven days. A person who returns to your site on Safari after eight days arrives with no ticket, and is counted as a new visitor.

Cookies set by the site's own server usually last longer, which is why many teams set the analytics cookie from their server. Safari limits some of those too, depending on how the server is set up. The details are covered in First-Party Cookies.

Local storage and session storage

Cookies are not the only place a site can keep values. Local storage keeps values in the browser until something clears them. Only JavaScript on the same site can read it, and it is never sent to the server with requests.

Session storage works the same way, but it belongs to one tab and is emptied when that tab closes. Developers use it for values that should not outlive the visit, such as a step in a form. Safari also clears script-written storage for sites a person has not used for seven days.

Looking at the cookies yourself

Open the browser's developer tools and choose the Application tab, called Storage in Firefox. Choose Cookies and then your site's domain. Each row shows a cookie's name, its value, its domain and its expiry date.

Find the Adobe cookie and read its domain and expiry. The domain should be your site's own. To test as a new visitor, delete that cookie, or open a private window, and load the page again. How to read the rest of these tools is covered in Developer tools and the network tab.

What this does to your numbers

Unique visitors are nearly always more than the real number of people, because one person with a phone, a laptop and a work computer counts as three. A tablet shared by a family is the exception, where several people count as one. The gap grows when browsers tighten their rules, because more tickets are lost. When a browser update shortens cookies, new visitors rise and returning visitors fall, and nobody's behaviour has changed.

Where this goes wrong

New visitors rise sharply in the month after a browser update. The rise is presented as a marketing success. The same people came back as before, but their cookies had been deleted, so they were counted again as new.

So before you explain a change in new or returning visitors, check two things: whether a browser changed its rules, and whether the site changed where or how it sets its cookies. Either one changes the count without changing the people.
