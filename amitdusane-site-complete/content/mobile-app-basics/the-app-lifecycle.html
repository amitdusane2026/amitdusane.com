Why it matters to you

A web page has two moments that matter: it loads, and it goes away. An app has neither. The states it moves through instead decide what can be measured at all, and they are the reason an app session and a website visit are not the same thing.

A page loads and unloads. An app does neither.

A web page has a clean life. It is requested, it loads, the reader does things, then they leave and it is destroyed. Nothing survives. The next page is a fresh load.

Analytics has hooked onto both ends of that for twenty years. Load is a page view, and leaving is the end of one.

An app is opened once and then stays. It stays in memory while somebody uses it. It stays in memory when they leave it. It is still there, holding everything it had, when they come back forty minutes later. There is no reload and no fresh start.

So the SDK hangs on different moments instead. Those moments are the lifecycle, and there are four states worth knowing by name.

The four states

Not runningInstalled, not in memory. Opening it now is a cold start: everything is built from nothing. Anything the app wrote to storage is still waiting.
ForegroundOn screen, being used. The only state in which anybody is looking at anything, and the only state that counts towards session length.
BackgroundOff screen, still in memory. Somebody took a call or locked the phone. Coming back is a warm start and is nearly instant. Session length stops counting while the app is here.
TerminatedGone from memory. Sometimes a person swiped it away. Far more often the operating system removed it to free memory. Nobody is told.

The hotel room you keep the key to

Think of the app as a hotel room with your things in it. While you are in the room, you are in the foreground. Step out for dinner and you are in the background. The room is untouched, your things are where you left them, and walking back in costs nothing. You did not check out.

At some point the room is reclaimed, and this is the part with no web equivalent. The hotel decides, not you. It happens while you are out, based on who needs the space more. Nobody phones to tell you. You find out the next time you go back, when you have to check in again and the room is empty.

That is exactly how termination works. The operating system takes memory back from apps that are not on screen whenever something in front needs it. The app is not asked and the person is not told.

Backgrounding pauses a session, it does not end it

Leaving the foreground stops the clock. The SDK notes the time and waits. Nothing is closed yet.

A new session begins only if the app stayed in the background for longer than the session timeout. That timeout is five minutes by default, and your team can change it in tags without touching the app.

So the small interruptions do not each create a session. A call, a message, a quick look at a train time: the person is back inside five minutes and the same session carries on. A twenty-minute shopping trip is usually one session.

What backgrounding does change is length. Session length counts foreground time only, so the minutes spent on that call are never added to it. The session continues and the clock does not.

Which report decides what a session means

The app does not decide. The SDK produces one count, your reporting tool produces another, and they are not the same number.

Adobe Analytics counts its own visits on the server, after the data has arrived. A visit ends after thirty minutes with no hit. That rule is the same for app data and website data, so an app has visits as well as launches.

Customer Journey Analytics does not fix the rule at all. You choose it yourself in the data view, and the choice covers every dataset in that view, website and app alike. One person on a laptop and a phone can be a single person there, inside a single session.

So find out which of the three a number is before explaining it. Most arguments about app sessions are two people holding two different metrics and believing they hold the same one.

The termination you will never see

You cannot reliably measure app closes. When the operating system terminates a backgrounded app, it does not run your code first. There is no final event, no chance to send anything, and no notification afterwards. The app stops existing, and the SDK was not told either.

This is why session length is measured from the foreground events rather than from anything at the end. The close you can see is the app leaving the foreground. What happens after that is invisible by design, on both platforms.

So anybody asking how many people closed the app deliberately is asking for a number that does not exist.

Where this goes wrong

A tracking plan asks for an app-close event. A developer builds one against the last hook the platform offers. It fires when somebody swipes the app away and stays silent for every operating-system termination, which is most of them. The number that arrives looks like a real count of people leaving. It is a partial count of one particular way of leaving.

What this does to your numbers

Three effects follow, and all three arrive without anybody writing a line of tracking code.

Launches are not installs. A launch is the first foreground moment after the app was not running, and also a return from the background past the session timeout. Installs are the first launch after installation, and upgrades the first after a version change. All of it arrives automatically from two events, switched on in Turn on lifecycle.

Launches are not visits either. A chart that puts SDK launches beside website visits is comparing two different measurements, whatever the labels say. Compare visits with visits, or build both sides from one Customer Journey Analytics data view, where the definition is yours and is the same for the app and the site. A screen is not a page view covers the other half of that mismatch.

Session length measures attention, not time in the app. The minutes an app spends in the background belong to nobody. That makes session length a stricter measure than time on site, and a smaller one, and it is correct rather than deflated.

What to tell people early

Most of the work here is setting expectations before anybody builds a report, rather than after somebody queries one.

SayWhich metric a number actually is: an SDK launch, an Adobe Analytics visit, or a session defined in a data view. Three different things, and most disagreements are two people holding two of them.
CheckWhat the session timeout is set to. Five minutes is the default, and a team that changed it has changed every session count in the app, retrospectively for nobody and going forward for everybody.
RefuseA reliable app close, or a “time until they quit” metric. The operating system terminates apps without running their code, so any number built for this counts one minority case and silently misses the rest.
