Why it matters to you

A screen and a page look alike in a report. They behave differently in real use, and the difference is where arguments about numbers that do not match come from.

What a page gives you that a screen does not

A web page comes with four things you have quietly relied on.

A URLSo it has a name nobody had to invent.
A referrerSo you know where the reader came from.
A loadOne moment that begins the page, and can be hooked.
An endIt is destroyed when the reader leaves, so the next one starts clean.

A screen has none of these. It has no address, so its name is whatever a developer decided to call it, which is very often the name of a class rather than anything a person would recognise. It has no referrer. It has no single load moment, because it can appear in several ways. And it is not destroyed when the person moves on, which is the difference that causes the most trouble.

The stack, and why going back is not a new view

Screens are arranged in a stack, like plates. Opening a product from a list puts the product screen on top. The list screen underneath is not gone. It is still in memory, still holding its scroll position, still holding whatever was typed into its search box.

Press back and the top plate is removed. The list is on screen again, exactly as it was. And here is the important part: it did not reload. Nothing was requested and nothing was rebuilt.

So if your tracking fires a screen view when a screen is created, that second appearance of the list produces nothing at all.

On the web this problem does not exist, because going back genuinely loads a page. In an app it has to be handled on purpose, by tracking the moment a screen appears rather than the moment it is built. Both platforms provide that moment. Somebody just has to choose it, and that is the decision Track screens and actions asks you to make.

A tracking plan that says “fire a screen view for each screen” has not made it.

Where this goes wrong

Screen views are wired to the moment each screen is built, which is the obvious place and the wrong one. Every screen reports correctly the first time and never again on the way back. Funnels look like people vanish at the second step, because returning to a list from a product is invisible. The fix needs a developer to move the call, so it is not something you can correct on the Adobe side.

Where sessions diverge

The second mismatch is the one that reaches stakeholders. Web visits and app sessions do not end for the same reason.

A web visit ends after thirty minutes of inactivity. It is a timer. Somebody leaves a tab open, makes a coffee, comes back inside half an hour, and is still in the same visit.

An app session ends when the app leaves the foreground, which is an event rather than a timer. Reading a message ends it. Taking a call ends it. Locking the phone ends it. Returning starts a new one immediately. The app lifecycle covers why that boundary sits there.

So app sessions are shorter and far more numerous than web visits, from the same behaviour by the same person. That is correct measurement rather than a fault, and it cannot be configured away on either side. Both numbers are right, and they answer different questions.

The comparison that will mislead everybody

Do not put app sessions and web visits in the same trend line. Not with a footnote, and not with a caveat in the title. The chart is the claim, and nobody reads the footnote.

They count different things. A line putting them side by side invites exactly one conclusion: either the app is more engaging or the website is collapsing. Neither is supported by the numbers underneath. The arithmetic is not wrong. What the chart implies is wrong, which is worse, because nothing on it admits that.

If somebody genuinely needs one number across both, that is a Customer Journey Analytics question. It needs the two datasets joined on a shared identity and a considered definition of what a session means across them. That is real work with a real answer, and it is not a chart you assemble in an afternoon.

Naming screens

Since a screen has no URL, somebody has to name it, and that somebody should be you.

The temptation is to reuse the website’s page names so the two line up in reporting. It looks tidy. It is really a promise that two products will stay matched, and neither team has made that promise.

Resist it. A screen that maps neatly onto a web page today will stop doing so at the next redesign, because the two products are redesigned separately. Apps also merge things the web splits: a mobile checkout is often one screen where a site has four pages. The mapping is not one to one even on the day you invent it.

Name screens for what they are in the app, using the words the product team uses in its own meetings. Those words will survive a redesign, and they are the words somebody will use when they ask you about the number.

Three decisions that are yours

SpecifyScreen views fire when a screen appears, not when it is built. It is the difference between counting a return to a list and losing it silently.
NameScreens after what they are in the app’s own language, not after website page names. The two products get redesigned separately, and a mapping made today survives neither.
AgreeApp sessions and web visits are never compared in one trend. Say it while it is a principle. After somebody has built the chart it becomes an argument.
