Here is a question that sounds trivial and, for years, could not be answered. A visitor sees a personalised offer, comes back the following week, and buys something. Did the offer work?

Answering it requires one thing: agreement that the person who saw the offer and the person who bought are the same person. Adobe Target knew about the first. Adobe Analytics knew about the second. Neither had any way of knowing they were discussing one human being, because each of them had invented its own name for the visitor and never told the other.

The Experience Cloud ID is the fix for that, and understanding what it actually identifies is the difference between reports you can trust and reports that quietly overcount every person who owns two devices.

The problem it was built to solve

Before a shared identifier existed, every Adobe solution minted its own. Analytics wrote a visitor ID into a cookie named s_vi. Target maintained its own identifier. Audience Manager had another. Each was perfectly correct within its own product, and each was useless outside it.

The consequence was not a reporting inconvenience. It made whole categories of question unanswerable. An audience built in one tool could not be measured in another. A test could not be connected to the conversion it produced. Every solution held a fragment of the same person and no two fragments could be joined.

One visitor, before and after a shared identifier

The ECID is issued at the organisation level rather than the product level. That single change is what makes a shared audience, a cross-solution report, and eventually Customer Journey Analytics possible at all.

What the ECID actually is, and where it lives

The identifier is produced by the Experience Cloud ID Service, delivered on the page by a library called VisitorAPI.js, and stored in cookies. Three of them matter, and knowing which does what turns most identity debugging into a thirty-second check.

CookiePartyWhat it holdsLifetime
AMCV_<org id>@AdobeOrgFirst-party, your domainThe ECID itself, as MCMID|<value>Persistent, subject to browser limits
AMCVS_<org id>@AdobeOrgFirst-party, your domainA session flag, value 1Ends with the session
demdexThird-party, on demdex.netAn Adobe-side ID used to recognise a returning visitor across sitesPersistent where third-party cookies survive at all

The important line in that table is the last one. The demdex cookie is the only third-party item, and third-party cookies are blocked by default in Safari and Firefox. Where that happens the service falls back to the first-party AMCV_ cookie alone, which still works perfectly well on your own domain and does nothing at all across domains. That single fact drives both Cross-Domain Tracking and First-Party Cookies.

It identifies a browser, not a person

This is the correction that matters most, and almost every misuse of Adobe Analytics data traces back to not having made it.

An ECID is written into a cookie. A cookie belongs to one browser on one device. So the identifier does not describe a human being, it describes a browser, and the moment those two things diverge your reports diverge with them.

One human being, three unique visitors
⚠️ Unique Visitors counts browsers, and it has always counted browsers

The metric is frequently read aloud in meetings as though it meant people, and it does not. A household sharing a tablet is one ECID covering several humans. One human with a phone, a work laptop and a private window is three ECIDs. Neither direction produces an error or a warning, and the number looks equally credible either way. If a business decision depends on a count of people rather than browsers, that count has to come from an authenticated identifier, not from this one.

setCustomerIDs, and the identity you actually control

The escape from the browser boundary is to supply an identifier of your own, at the one moment you genuinely know who somebody is: when they sign in.

The ID service accepts customer identifiers through setCustomerIDs, which associates your own value with the ECID currently in play. Sign in on a phone and again on a laptop, and both ECIDs become associated with the same customer identifier, which is what allows the two devices to be recognised as one person downstream.

What you pass matters, and the rule from Data Layer Design applies directly: use your own internal identifier, never a raw email address or phone number. The identifier still identifies a person once joined to your systems, which is precisely why it is useful and precisely why it carries the labeling obligation described in Privacy and Data Retention.

💡 Set the customer ID on every authenticated page, not only at sign-in

Sign-in happens once, and the session that follows can run for hours across many page views. Sending the identifier only on the login page means every subsequent hit carries the browser identity alone, and any device-stitching that depended on it applies to one page view rather than the whole visit. If the visitor is authenticated, say so on every hit.

The load order that breaks implementations without saying so

Now the operational detail that has cost more implementations than any other in this section, and it is not conceptual at all.

VisitorAPI.js must execute before AppMeasurement. Adobe places it at the beginning of the head, ahead of every other solution's code, and the reason is mechanical: AppMeasurement asks the ID service for an identity when it builds a request. If the ID service has not finished initialising, there is nothing to ask.

The order that has to hold
<head>
    VisitorAPI.js          first, always
    AppMeasurement.js      after it, never before
    other solution code
</head>

In Launch this ordering is handled by the extensions.
It breaks when hand-placed code is mixed with a tag manager,
or when one library is loaded async and the other is not.
⚠️ The hit still goes out, which is why nobody notices

A request built before the ID service is ready does not fail. It is sent, it is processed, and it appears in reports carrying a different identity from every other hit in the same visit. The visit fragments, unique visitors inflate, and the pages that happen to load fastest are the ones affected. There is no console error, no failed request in the network tab, and no report that highlights it. The symptom is a visitor count that is slightly too high forever.

Coming from the legacy Analytics ID, and the grace period nobody plans for

Implementations that predate the ID service already identified visitors, using the Analytics visitor ID stored in the s_vi cookie, often called the AAID. Deploying the ID service on top of that history raises an obvious question: what happens to everyone already being tracked?

The service handles it rather than discarding it. Where an s_vi cookie exists, the existing Analytics ID is written into the AMCV_ cookie alongside a newly issued ECID, so both identifiers travel together. The legacy ID sits higher in the order of operations, so it continues to be the visitor identifier until the AMCV_ cookie expires or is cleared. Existing visitors keep their history instead of appearing as a wave of new people on deployment day.

ℹ️ The grace period exists for partial rollouts, and it has a clock

Rolling the ID service out across a large estate rarely happens in one night, and a half-migrated site creates a specific problem: pages with the service and pages without it, all reporting into the same report suite, disagreeing about who the visitor is. Adobe Customer Care can enable a grace period, during which the service returns both identifiers so the two kinds of page continue to agree. It runs for up to 180 days and can be renewed. It is meant to be switched off once every page reporting into that suite has the service deployed, and a grace period quietly left running long after the migration finished is a loose end worth checking for.

The same identity, without the library

Implementations on the Web SDK do not load VisitorAPI.js at all. Identity is managed by the Edge Network, which issues and returns the ECID as part of handling the request, so the load-order problem above simply does not exist in that architecture.

What does not change is the concept. It is still an organisation-level identifier, still tied to a browser rather than a person, still dependent on a first-party cookie surviving, and still improved enormously by supplying an authenticated identifier of your own. Moving to the Web SDK changes which component holds the responsibility. It does not change what the identifier means or what it cannot tell you.

What you have now

The ECID exists because identity used to belong to products rather than to organisations, and that made the most valuable questions unanswerable. One shared identifier at the organisation level is what turned a set of separate tools into something that can describe a single journey.

The part to carry away is what it identifies. Not a person, a browser. Every unique visitor count you have ever quoted is a count of browsers, and the gap between those two things widens with every device your customers own. Closing it requires an identifier you control, supplied when somebody signs in, on every authenticated hit rather than only the first.

Around that sit two failure modes that never announce themselves: a library load order that produces perfectly valid hits carrying the wrong identity, and a grace period left running years after the migration it was created for.

All of this holds while the visitor stays on one domain, because the cookie carrying the ECID is readable only by the domain that wrote it. The moment a journey crosses to a domain you also own, that identifier does not travel and the visitor becomes two strangers. Cross-Domain Tracking covers how to carry it across on purpose.

Where to find it in Adobe Launch

Install and configure identity: open the property, then Extensions > Experience Cloud ID Service, where the organisation ID is set and customer ID variables are declared.

Verify a live ECID: open the browser console and inspect the AMCV_<org id>@AdobeOrg cookie, or read the ECID directly with the Adobe Experience Platform Debugger.
