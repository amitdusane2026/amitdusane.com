An email arrives from somebody in legal. A person has exercised a right they hold under law: tell me what you have collected about me, and then delete it. There is a deadline attached, it is counted in days rather than months, and the answer has to be accurate rather than reassuring.

Nothing in that request is an analytics question. Answering it, however, depends entirely on decisions taken inside a report suite, most of them years earlier, by somebody who was thinking about conversion rates at the time. Whether the data still exists. Whether Adobe has any idea which of your two hundred variables happens to hold an email address. Whether a person who arrived as an email address can be found in data that recognises visitors by a cookie.

Those three questions are the whole of this section, and the order they are asked in matters, because each one decides whether the next gets asked at all.

Retention and privacy are not two subjects, they are a sequence

They sit next to each other in the admin tools and they are usually treated as two unrelated pieces of housekeeping: one about storage, one about compliance. That reading survives right up until the first request arrives, because Adobe connects them explicitly. A data retention policy has to be in place before Adobe will process a data privacy request at all.

The dependency makes sense once you look at what a deletion promise actually requires. To state that a person has been erased, there has to be an agreed edge to the data being searched. An unbounded archive has no edge, so there is nothing to make a promise about. Retention supplies the boundary, and only then does the machinery that finds and anonymises a person inside that boundary have somewhere to operate.

Three gates, and a request has to pass all of them

Read from left to right, the diagram is a compliance workflow. Read from right to left, it is a list of things that have to be decided at implementation time, by people who will not be in the room when the email from legal arrives.

The one setting in this module you are not permitted to change

Adobe applies a default data retention policy of 25 months. Your contract may say something different, and whatever it says is what governs, but 25 months is where an organisation that has never discussed the question finds itself.

The interesting part is what you can do about it, which is almost nothing on your own. You can open the data governance dialog in the admin tools and read the policy currently in force. Changing it is a conversation with your Adobe account team. Reducing the period costs nothing. Extending it beyond the default is a purchase, made in yearly increments, with eight extensions available at most, which puts the ceiling at ten years and one month.

ℹ️ What you can see, and what you have to ask for

The policy is visible to you and editable by Adobe. That split is easy to misread as a gap in the product, and administrators go looking for the dropdown that would let them fix it. There is no dropdown. If the retention period is wrong for your organisation, the route is commercial rather than technical, and it runs through whoever owns the Adobe relationship.

It is worth sitting with the design decision for a moment, because it runs directly against the grain of everything else in this panel. Bot Filtering hands you a checkbox and steps back, on the reasoning that deciding which traffic counts is your judgment to make and defend. Retention is the same product, in the same screen, taking the opposite position.

The difference is what happens when the decision is wrong. An unticked bot rule leaves noise in a report, and the report is still there to be corrected later. A retention period set too short begins destroying data on a schedule, permanently, and no support ticket brings it back. A control whose worst outcome is total and irreversible does not belong behind a dropdown that a newly promoted administrator can reach on a Tuesday afternoon. The friction is not an oversight. The friction is the safety mechanism.

What deleted means here, and when you find out

Deletion runs as a rolling window rather than as an event. The window starts at today minus your retention period and ends at today, and everything inside it is available. Everything outside it is not, and the boundary steps forward on a monthly cycle, quietly, in the background, taking another month with it each time.

The window does not sit still

What leaves is not archived to somewhere cheaper. Adobe reserves the right to delete data that has passed the policy, with no option for recovery, and that applies uniformly to everything downstream: Analysis Workspace, Data Warehouse, and the data feeds that carry raw hits into your own systems. If the window has passed, the question cannot be answered by any surface Adobe offers.

One escape hatch exists, and it has to be used before the fact rather than after. A historical data dump can be requested ahead of a deletion, which turns data you are about to lose into a file you own. It is only available while the data is still there.

⚠️ Nothing announces the day your history stops

There is no notification, no banner, and no entry in any log a practitioner would normally read. The first visible symptom is a year over year comparison that returns nothing for the earlier period, and in a Workspace panel an absent period and a catastrophic one render identically: both are zero. The person who discovers the retention boundary is usually the person presenting the trend, and they will discover it in front of an audience who assume the number means what it appears to mean.

Choosing a period, and why it is not an analytics decision

Left to analysts, retention would always be set to the maximum, on the entirely reasonable grounds that data you no longer hold is data you can never analyse. That instinct is worth naming, because two other departments have standing to overrule it and both of them are usually right.

Legal sets a floor and a ceiling at the same time. Certain obligations require records to be kept for a defined period. Purpose limitation, the principle underneath GDPR that says personal data is held for a stated reason and no longer than that reason survives, pushes hard in the other direction. Under that principle, an unnecessarily long retention period is not a prudent hedge. It is exposure, deliberately created and documented.

Finance sets the price. Reductions are free, extensions are bought, and the argument for a fourth year of history has to survive a renewal conversation.

Retention windowWhat it actually supportsWhat usually drives it
6 to 12 monthsCurrent trading only. No comparison against the same period last year is possible at any point in the calendarA strict purpose-limitation position, or cost
13 monthsOne year-on-year point: this month measured against the same month last year, and nothing widerThe practical floor for anyone reporting on seasonality
25 monthsA complete year compared against a complete prior year, with the current year still running underneath itAdobe's default, and the answer most organisations settle on

The table is worth reading as a one-way door. Every other setting in this module can be changed later at the cost of a seam in the data. Shortening retention changes what exists, and lengthening it afterwards does not bring back what the shorter window already discarded. The decision runs in one direction only.

💡 Record the comparison that justifies the window, next to the window

A retention period defended as "two years feels right" loses to the first cost review that asks the question directly, because a feeling does not survive a spreadsheet. A period defended as "the board pack compares each quarter against the same quarter last year, which needs 25 months" survives, because it names something the business would visibly lose. Write down the specific comparison the window exists to support, and keep it wherever the rest of the suite's decisions live.

IP obfuscation costs far less than almost everyone assumes

The visitor IP address is the most obviously personal thing an analytics implementation touches, and the control governing it sits in general account settings with three positions. Disabled leaves the address in place. Obfuscate replaces it with two colons followed by a hashed value. Remove replaces it with a placeholder, and remove is the option selected by default on every newly created report suite.

The widespread belief about this setting is that switching it on is a trade: privacy purchased at the cost of your geographic reporting, your bot rules, and your ability to exclude traffic by address. The belief is reasonable, it is the intuition almost everyone arrives at, and the documented behaviour contradicts it.

Obfuscation happens last. IP filtering and exclusion, bot rule evaluation, and geo-segmentation lookups are all completed before the address is touched. Country, region and city resolve normally. The exclude-by-IP tool described in Bot Filtering, which is the practical answer to automated traffic the IAB list never catches, keeps working exactly as it did. Nothing in your reporting goes dark.

What you give up is narrower, and it is worth stating precisely: the raw address is not retained, so anything needing the address after processing has finished does not have it. That is not a reporting limitation. It is an investigative one.

⚠️ You cannot exclude an address you can no longer see

The usual sequence for finding unwanted automated traffic is to notice a pattern in last quarter's data, identify the address behind it, and add an exclusion. With the address removed at collection, the middle step is gone: the pattern is visible and its origin is not. The exclusion can only be built from traffic arriving now. Like every other setting in this panel the change is forward-only, so a suite switched to remove keeps whatever addresses it already stored and never records another.

Adobe has no idea what you put in eVar 12

Here is the assumption that quietly defeats first-time compliance work. Adobe Analytics knows the shape of your data completely and its meaning not at all. It knows eVar 12 exists, that it holds strings, and how many unique values arrived last month. Whether those strings are product categories or customer email addresses is knowledge held inside your organisation, sometimes inside one person, and never inside the platform.

Privacy labeling is the act of writing that knowledge down in a form the Privacy Service can execute against. It is the translation layer between your schema and a legal category, and until it exists, a request has nothing to act on.

LabelWhat it declares about the fieldDependency
I1Identifies a person directly: a name, an email address, a phone numberPrerequisite for the ID and DEL labels

The dependency column is the part to internalise. Identity labels are load-bearing: without I1 or I2 on a field, the delete label cannot be attached to it at all. Somebody who labels the obvious personal fields as sensitive and stops there has built a taxonomy that describes the data accurately and instructs the platform to do nothing.

One variable group at a time, one report suite at a time. Labels belong to the suite they were applied in, exactly like bot rules, IP exclusions, and everything else in this module, which means a suite created next year for a new market starts with none of them.

A request that finds nothing looks exactly like a request that worked

Consider what the Privacy Service does when it reaches a variable carrying an email address and no labels. It does not fail. It does not warn. It has been told nothing about that field, so it looks at nothing, finds nothing, and reports that the work is complete.

The access request returns a small, clean file. The delete request finishes and is recorded as fulfilled. Both responses are entirely honest about what they did, and completely silent about the field they were never told to look at. To everyone in the chain, from the analyst who ran it to the legal team who filed the response, the transaction looks like a success.

⚠️ The gap surfaces years later, and never at a convenient moment

An unlabeled variable produces no error at the time and no artefact afterwards, which means the failure has no discovery mechanism of its own. It waits. What eventually surfaces it is an audit, a regulator's follow-up question, or a second request from a person who has noticed their data is still being used, and by then the original response has been sent, logged, and relied upon. Labeling is checked by opening the screen and reading it, because nothing else in the system will ever raise a hand.

You cannot erase a person you cannot name

The last gate is the one that catches implementations which have done everything else correctly. A request arrives carrying an email address, because that is how the person knows themselves. Adobe Analytics does not recognise anybody by email address. It recognises the Experience Cloud ID, the legacy Analytics cookie that preceded it, and a custom visitor ID if the implementation was built to set one.

So the request has to be translated before it can be submitted, and there are two routes. The first is to hold a person-level identifier in the data already, a CRM identifier in a custom visitor ID or a labeled eVar, which lets the email be resolved to something Adobe understands using systems you control. The second is to collect the device identifiers straight from the browser at the moment the person asks, which is what Adobe's unified privacy JavaScript exists to do.

The two routes are not equivalent, and the difference decides how much you can actually honour. Retrieving identifiers from the browser only reaches the browser in front of you. It cannot reach the phone the same person used last year, or the laptop they have since replaced. A person-level identifier collected at authentication reaches all of them, because it was the same value every time they signed in.

💡 Settle the identity route while nobody is waiting for an answer

This is the one part of privacy readiness that cannot be assembled after a request arrives, because the answer depends on data collected months or years earlier. Establish which identifier a request will be matched against, confirm it is being captured, and confirm it is labeled, as a piece of implementation work with no deadline attached. A statutory clock is a poor environment for discovering that the identifier was never collected.

What deletion then performs is worth understanding, because the word oversells it. Values are anonymised rather than removed. Traffic and commerce variables are overwritten with a random hexadecimal string prefixed with "Data Privacy-", the visitor ID is replaced with a random value, custom visitor IDs and IP addresses are cleared, and precise coordinates are degraded to roughly a kilometre.

The hit itself survives all of this. Visits, page views and revenue do not move when a deletion is fulfilled, and reports run the next morning look exactly as they did the night before. That is deliberate, and it resolves a tension that would otherwise be unmanageable: the individual becomes unfindable while the aggregate remains true. Honouring a person's rights does not require falsifying your own history.

What you have now

Three gates, arranged in an order you do not control. Retention decides whether the data is still there, and it is set by contract rather than by an administrator, because the consequence of getting it wrong is permanent. Labeling decides whether Adobe can locate personal data inside your schema, and it is applied by hand, per report suite, by somebody who knows what the variables were actually used for. Identity decides whether the person in the request can be matched to the person in the data, and that depends on a collection decision taken long before anyone asked.

Every one of the three fails quietly. History that passes the retention window leaves no notice. A missing label produces a successful-looking response. An unmatched identity returns an empty result rather than an error. This is the same property that runs under every setting in this module, but with the consequences moved outside analytics entirely, into a domain where the questions are asked by regulators and the answers are already on record.

All of this governs data that has already been collected. The question of whether it should have been collected at all belongs to the visitor, is expressed through a consent choice, and has to be honoured before a single tag fires. Consent and Tag Management covers how a tag management system reads that choice and refuses to act without it.

Where to find it in Adobe Analytics

Privacy labeling: Admin > All admin > Data configuration and collection > Data governance, then select the report suite. The current retention policy is displayed here as well, for reading only; changing it goes through your Adobe account team.

IP obfuscation: Admin > Report Suites > Edit Settings > General > General Account Settings. Set per report suite, and forward-only, like the rest of that panel.
