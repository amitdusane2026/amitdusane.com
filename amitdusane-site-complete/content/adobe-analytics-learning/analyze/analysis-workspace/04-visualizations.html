Nobody has ever been persuaded by a table.

The table is where the answer is decided, and it is almost never where the answer is delivered. Somewhere between the analysis and the meeting, a number has to become a shape that a person absorbs in two seconds while half-listening. That conversion is what visualizations are for, and it is a genuinely different job from analysis.

It also has a mechanic underneath it that catches almost everybody once, because a chart in this product is not the independent object it appears to be.

Every chart is a drawing of a table, including the ones that look standalone

Drop a line chart into a panel and it appears with data already in it, which feels like magic and is not. The visualization has attached itself to the nearest freeform table and is drawing that table's numbers.

It has no data of its own. It never had. It is a rendering, and the table is the thing.

📊 One table, three pictures, no independent data anywhere

That is mostly a gift. It means a chart is never out of step with the analysis behind it, and it means you fix a wrong picture by fixing the table rather than by rebuilding the picture.

It also means every warning in Freeform Tables applies to charts without being repeated. A chart drawn from a breakdown is showing a breakdown's numbers, scoped to the parent row, with no indentation left to warn anybody.

The data source setting, and the chart that goes blank for no reason

Because a visualization attaches to the nearest table, "nearest" turns into a rule you did not choose and will eventually collide with.

Add a second table above the chart and the chart may be reading something new. Delete the table a chart was drawing from, and the chart empties. Collapse or move things around while tidying a project, and a picture that was right yesterday now shows something else, with no error and no complaint, because every state involved is legitimate.

The fix is the Data Source tab in the visualization's own settings, which shows exactly which table and which rows are feeding it, and lets you point it somewhere deliberate rather than accepting proximity.

⚠️ Deleting a table quietly empties every chart that was drawing from it

The link between a chart and its table is invisible on the canvas. Nothing draws a line between them, and a chart does not announce which table it belongs to. So the ordinary act of cleaning up a project, removing a working table that was only ever scaffolding, takes the charts with it. They do not error. They go blank, or worse, silently re-attach to whichever table is now nearest and carry on displaying confident numbers about a different subject. Before deleting any table, check the Data Source tab of every visualization near it.

Pick the shape from the question, not from the catalogue

There are around two dozen visualization types, and the number is misleading, because most analysis is served by about five of them. The catalogue rewards browsing and punishes it: the temptation is to pick something that looks impressive, and impressive is not a property a chart should have.

The useful way to choose is to say the sentence out loud first, then take the shape that matches its verb.

The sentenceThe shapeWhy that one
"This is going up, and here is when it turned"LineTime on the horizontal axis is the only thing people read without instruction
"These things are not the same size"Bar, or horizontal bar for long labelsLength is the one visual property humans compare accurately
"One number matters more than the rest"Summary number or summary changeA chart of one value is a chart wasted
"These two groups overlap more than you think"VennThe only shape that shows membership in two sets at once
"We are tracking against a target"BulletBuilt for actual against goal, which a bar cannot express
"Most of the total comes from a few items"Treemap or barDonuts are worse than bars at exactly this and get used anyway
"Where did people go, and where did they stop"Flow, FalloutCovered in Fallout and Flow Analysis

The opinion worth holding, for a first pass: line for time, bar for comparison, summary number for the headline, and stop. That covers the large majority of what a business report needs. It is scaffolding rather than law, and the moment you can say precisely why a scatter or a histogram answers your question better, use it and ignore the rule.

The one to be actively suspicious of is the donut. It asks the reader to compare angles, which people are measurably poor at, and it stops working entirely beyond about four slices. A horizontal bar answers the same question and can be read from the back of a room.

Three of them are not charts, and they are what makes a project readable

Buried in the same catalogue are components that draw no data at all, and they matter more than their position in the list suggests.

Text puts a paragraph on the canvas. Section header puts a heading on it. Neither does anything analytical, and together they are the difference between a project a colleague can read alone and a project that requires you standing next to it explaining what they are looking at.

This is worth taking seriously, because the failure mode is so ordinary. A project built in an afternoon of genuine investigation is perfectly legible to the person who built it and close to meaningless to anybody else, and the gap is never the data. It is that nothing on the canvas says what question was being asked or what the answer turned out to be.

💡 Write the finding above the chart that proves it

A text block above each panel saying what the panel found, in one sentence, in plain language, does more for a project's usefulness than any chart choice. "Mobile converts at half the rate of desktop, and the gap is entirely in checkout" is a finding. A bar chart is evidence for it. Most projects ship the evidence and leave the finding in the analyst's head, which is why so many of them get opened once. It also ages well: in six months the sentence is what tells you whether the project is still worth keeping.

Follow along: one table, three pictures, then break it on purpose

The mechanic in this section is easier to trust once you have watched it happen, particularly the part where a chart silently changes what it is about.

Follow along: prove that the chart is a drawing of the table
PART ONE, ATTACHMENT

1  A panel with one freeform table: Day in rows, Page Views in columns

2  Left rail > Visualizations > drag a Line chart under the table
       It arrives already populated. It found the table itself.

3  Add a Bar chart and a Summary number the same way

4  Now change the table: swap the metric, or add a segment to a column
       All three pictures move together. None of them was edited.

PART TWO, THE BREAK

5  Insert a second freeform table ABOVE one of the charts
       Check whether that chart is still reading the table you
       think it is.

6  Open the chart's settings > Data Source
       This is the only place the attachment is visible. Point it
       deliberately at the table you actually mean.

7  Delete the original table
       Watch what happens to the charts that were drawing from it.
       Undo immediately, and remember the shape of that failure.

PART THREE, MAKE IT READABLE

8  Left rail > Visualizations > Text, dropped above the panel
       Write one sentence saying what the panel found. Not what
       it shows. What it found.

There is nothing else to configure. A visualization has almost no
settings of its own, because almost every decision was already made
in the table it is drawing.

Those eight steps are a floor rather than a ceiling. Step 7 is the one worth doing deliberately, in a project nobody depends on, so that the first time a chart empties is a choice rather than an emergency.

What you have now

Every visualization in Analysis Workspace is a drawing of a freeform table. It holds no data, inherits every decision the table made, attaches itself to whichever table is nearest unless told otherwise, and empties without complaint if that table disappears. The Data Source tab is the only place that relationship is visible, and checking it is what separates a project that survives tidying from one that quietly starts lying.

Choosing among two dozen shapes matters less than it appears to. Line for time, bar for comparison, a summary number for the headline, a sentence of text saying what was found. Most of what makes a project persuasive is not in the catalogue at all.

Four of the entries in that list are different in kind, though. They do not draw an existing table, they perform an analysis of their own, and each answers a question a freeform table structurally cannot. Cohort Analysis is the first: it stops asking what happened this month and starts asking whether the people who arrived in January were ever seen again.

Where to find it in Adobe Analytics

Analytics > Workspace, then the Visualizations icon, second in the left rail. Drag any entry onto the canvas, or use Insert in the menu bar.

Every visualization carries a gear for its own settings. Data Source inside those settings is where the link to a freeform table is shown and changed, and it is the first place to look when a chart shows something unexpected.
