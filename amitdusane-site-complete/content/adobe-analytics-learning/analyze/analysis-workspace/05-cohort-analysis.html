A gym in January is the busiest room in the country. Memberships climb, the floor is full, and every number the manager looks at is up.

By March the same gym is quiet, and the reports still look fine. New joiners keep arriving, total members holds steady, revenue is flat rather than falling. Nothing on the dashboard says that almost nobody who joined in January is still coming, because the arrivals are covering for the departures.

Finding that out requires a different question. Not how many people are here, but what happened to a specific group of people over time. That question has a shape of its own, and a freeform table cannot express it.

A cohort is a group defined by when, not by who

Most of analytics separates people by attribute. Which channel they came from, which country they are in, which device they used. A segment is exactly that: a description of who somebody is or what they did.

A cohort is different. It groups people by when they first did something, and then follows that group forward through time regardless of anything else about them. Everyone who first purchased in week one is a cohort. Everyone who first purchased in week two is a different cohort, and the two are never mixed again.

That single change is what makes decline visible. Totals hide it, because a total is a snapshot with new arrivals blended in. Follow one group and there is nowhere for the loss to hide.

Two criteria, and confusing them is the standard mistake

A cohort table asks you for two things, and they sound similar enough that people set both to the same value and then wonder why the first column is always one hundred percent.

Inclusion criteria decides who gets into the cohort and when their clock starts. Return criteria decides what counts as coming back. They are separate questions and they are frequently different actions.

📊 One event puts you in the group. A different event proves you stayed.

Two readings come free from that grid and both are worth naming.

Reading across a row shows the life of one group: how quickly a single intake fell away. Reading down a column compares intakes at the same age, which is the only fair comparison and the one people skip. January's cohort at week three against February's cohort at week three is a real question. January's cohort at week eight against February's at week two is not a comparison at all, and the triangular shape of the table invites exactly that mistake.

Three tables wearing the same grid

The table type changes what the grid measures without changing how it looks, which is worth knowing before somebody hands you one.

TypeWhat each cell saysReach for it when
RetentionHow many of the cohort met the return criteria in this periodThe default question: did they stay
ChurnThe inverse: how many did not returnThe number has to land in a meeting. Sixty percent lost reads harder than forty percent kept
LatencyTime before and after the inclusion event, with inclusion in the middleYou want to know what people did in the run-up, not only afterward

Latency is the one most people never open and the one that most often changes a decision. It puts the inclusion event at the center and shows the periods on both sides, so it answers what somebody was doing before they converted as well as after. That is the half of the journey most reporting throws away.

Rolling calculation moves the denominator, and the numbers get better

There is a switch called rolling calculation, and it is the one setting in this visualization that can flatter a decline into something acceptable.

By default, every percentage is measured against the original cohort. Week three is a share of everybody who joined. With rolling calculation on, each period is measured against the period before it instead.

Both are legitimate and they answer different questions. Against the original cohort is survival: how much of the intake is left. Against the previous period is period-over-period retention: of the people still here last week, how many are still here now. The second is almost always the higher number, because it forgives everyone who already left.

📊 Identical people, identical weeks, and the percentages climb
⚠️ Rolling calculation makes retention look better and does not say so

Switch it on and every figure after the first column improves, because the denominator shrinks to match the survivors rather than the intake. Nothing in the table announces which mode is active, and both produce a plausible-looking percentage in the same cell. A cohort quoted at "sixty-eight percent retention at week four" means something completely different under the two settings, and the flattering reading is the one that gets into a deck. Whenever a retention figure is presented, ask what the denominator is before asking whether the number is good.

What the cohort table refuses to accept

This visualization is fussier about metrics than anything else in Workspace, and discovering that mid-build is a familiar frustration.

It takes only metrics it can filter people by, which rules out several things you will reach for on instinct:

RefusedWhy
Calculated metricsThey are evaluated after rows are assembled, so there is nothing to test a person against
Revenue and other non-integer metricsA cohort asks whether somebody did a thing, not how much the thing was worth
OccurrencesNot a filterable action

The calculated metric exclusion is the one that stings, because the natural instinct is to build a cohort on conversion rate, and conversion rate is a ratio rather than an action. The way through is to reach for the underlying event instead: not "converted at some rate" but "placed an order". Calculated Metrics covers why a formula cannot reach back to an individual person, and cohort tables are one of the five places that boundary is visible.

Cohorts do not have to be about time

The default reading of a cohort is temporal, and it is not the only one available. A cohort can be built on a dimension instead: everybody who first arrived through paid search, everybody whose first purchase was in a particular category, everybody who started on mobile.

That turns the grid into something more useful than a retention curve. It stops asking how long people stay and starts asking which kind of first experience produces people who stay. Those are the same numbers pointed at an acquisition decision rather than a loyalty one, and the second is usually the one somebody is about to spend money on.

💡 The comparison that pays for the whole exercise

Build the same cohort table twice, split by first acquisition channel. Retention curves by channel almost always diverge more sharply than acquisition volumes do, and the channel that looks cheapest per visit is regularly the one whose people are gone by week two. That single comparison reframes a budget conversation from cost per acquisition to cost per acquisition that lasted, and it is difficult to argue with because it follows named groups rather than averages.

Those nine steps are a floor rather than a ceiling. Step 8 in particular is worth doing once with a real dataset, because seeing your own numbers improve without anything improving is more persuasive than any warning about denominators.

What you have now

A cohort groups people by when they first did something and follows that group forward, which is the only arrangement in this product that makes a slow loss visible. Inclusion criteria starts the clock, return criteria decides what counts as coming back, and setting them to the same thing produces a table that flatters you. Retention, churn and latency are three readings of one grid. Rolling calculation quietly changes the denominator and improves every number after the first.

The reason it matters beyond retention reporting is the habit it builds. Almost every reassuring number in analytics is a total, and almost every total is new arrivals covering for departures. Once you have seen a cohort table disagree with a healthy-looking trend line, you stop trusting aggregates on their own, and that suspicion is worth more than the visualization.

Cohorts answer whether people came back. They say nothing about what those people did while they were there, or where the ones who left gave up. Fallout and Flow Analysis covers both: one tests a path you already believe in, and the other shows you the paths you never thought to look for.

Where to find it in Adobe Analytics

Analytics > Workspace, then the Visualizations icon in the left rail, and Cohort table. It can also be added from Insert in the menu bar.

Inclusion criteria, return criteria, granularity and the table type all sit in the configuration panel that appears before the table is built. The gear on a finished cohort table reopens them, including Rolling calculation and the latency option.
