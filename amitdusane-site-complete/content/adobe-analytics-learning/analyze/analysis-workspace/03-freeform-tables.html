A freeform table looks like the least interesting thing on the screen. It is a grid. Rows down the side, numbers in the middle, nothing that would survive a slide.

It is also where every number in Analysis Workspace is decided. The charts are drawings of it. The fallouts and the cohorts are specialized versions of it. And almost every confidently wrong figure this product has ever produced came out of a table that was arranged slightly differently from the way the person reading it assumed.

Which makes this the one component worth understanding properly rather than operating.

Rows ask what, columns ask how much

The grammar is small enough to state in a sentence. A dimension in the rows names the things you want separated. A metric in the columns says how those things are measured. Drop Page into the rows and Page Views into the columns, and you have asked for page views, separated by page.

Everything else in this section is what happens when that sentence gets more than one clause.

📊 The whole grammar, before anything gets complicated

A breakdown is a second question asked inside the first

Here is the mechanic that produces more misread reports than anything else in the product, and it is disguised as an obvious convenience.

Take that table and break the product detail row down by Browser. Numbers appear underneath it, indented. It looks like you have added a second dimension to the table.

You have not. You have asked a completely new question: which browsers were used, among the page views of that one page. The breakdown is scoped inside the row above it. It is not a list of browsers, it is a list of browsers for that page, and the numbers underneath will never sum to the site's browser totals.

That distinction sounds pedantic until somebody screenshots the indented rows, drops them into a deck, and labels them "browser usage".

📊 The same two dimensions, swapped, are two different reports

Both tables are correct. They answer different questions, and the only visible difference is which dimension was dragged first. A colleague handed the wrong one will read it fluently and reach the wrong conclusion without ever noticing there was a choice.

⚠️ Indented rows never sum to the site total, and they look exactly like rows that do

A breakdown inherits every filter above it, including the parent row itself. So the numbers under a broken-down row are scoped to that row and nothing else. Copy them into a slide and they become a claim about the whole site, which is a claim the table never made. The tell is indentation, and indentation survives neither a screenshot nor a paste into a spreadsheet. If a figure is going to travel, take it from a table where the dimension sits at the top level, not from a breakdown.

Attribution is a column setting, and it is where props stop being second class

Every column carries an attribution model, whether or not anybody chose one. Left alone, a metric is credited according to the dimension's own behavior, which is why a prop broken down by orders normally reports almost nothing: a prop holds its value for a single hit, so only the hit that carried the order can receive credit.

Open the column settings, apply an attribution model, and that stops being true.

📊 Same visit, same order, and the model decides who gets credit

Two things follow from that, and both are larger than a column setting has any right to be.

The first is that the distinction between props and eVars, which governs so much implementation planning, does not survive contact with an attribution model. Applied at report time, a prop can carry any lookback window you choose, and an eVar's configured allocation and expiration are set aside entirely.

The second is that a decision you thought was permanent turns out not to be. An eVar's expiry is chosen on the day it is implemented and lived with for years. Here the same question is a dropdown, answerable differently by two people looking at the same data on the same afternoon. What Is Attribution takes the models themselves apart.

Dynamic rows keep re-asking, static rows freeze the answer

Drag a dimension into the rows and the table fills with whatever the top values happen to be. Those rows are dynamic: they are a standing instruction to fetch the top items, re-evaluated every time the report runs.

That is usually what you want, and occasionally a quiet disaster. A project built in March showing the top ten products, opened in September, is showing September's top ten. The chart has the same title and a different subject, and nobody scrolling past it will suspect anything, because it looks exactly as it did.

Dragging a specific dimension item instead pins it. That row is now static: it will report that one value forever, and will show zero rather than silently substituting a replacement.

💡 Anything a stakeholder will read monthly should be pinned

The rule that avoids most of this: dynamic rows for exploring, static rows for anything scheduled, shared, or repeated. If a project goes to the same five people every month, they are reading it for change over time, and change over time is meaningless if the rows themselves change underneath the numbers. Pinning also makes a disappearance visible, because a static row that drops to zero is a finding, while a dynamic row that quietly falls out of the top ten is nothing at all.

Percentages are honest about the number and quiet about the denominator

A percentage column looks like the least ambiguous thing on the page. It is a share, and shares are simple.

The question a percentage never answers on screen is a share of what. Within a breakdown, the percentage is of the parent row. At the top level, it is of the column. Change the arrangement, and the same visual percentage measures something else entirely while looking identical.

This compounds with the breakdown problem, because the most quotable figure in any table is a percentage inside a breakdown, and that is exactly the one whose denominator is invisible.

Follow along: build one table four ways and watch the meaning move

This is the exercise that makes the rest of this section permanent, and it takes about five minutes. The point is not the numbers. It is watching how little has to change for a number to mean something else.

Follow along: four arrangements, four different questions
SETUP

1  A blank panel, a report suite with data, a month that has some

ARRANGEMENT ONE, the plain question

2  Rows: Browser.  Columns: Page Views.
       Note the number against the top browser. Write it down.

ARRANGEMENT TWO, the breakdown

3  Right-click the top browser row > Break down > Day
       The indented numbers now sum to that browser's total,
       not to the site's daily totals. Same data, narrower question.

ARRANGEMENT THREE, the swap

4  New table. Rows: Day.  Break the top day down by Browser.
       Find the cell that matches step 3. It is the only figure
       the two tables share. Everything around it has changed.

ARRANGEMENT FOUR, pinned

5  From the left rail, expand the Browser dimension and drag one
   specific browser into the rows, instead of the dimension itself
       That row is now static. It will report that browser next
       year, or report zero, and will never quietly become a
       different browser.

THE COLUMN SETTING

6  Column settings on a conversion metric > Attribution > Last touch
       If the row dimension is a prop, watch the numbers move.
       Nothing was recollected. The model was recalculated.

There is nothing else to configure. Every table in this product is
these few settings in some combination.

Those six steps are a floor rather than a ceiling. Step 3 and step 4 together are the ones worth repeating until the difference is obvious, because that pair is the difference between reading a table and operating one.

What you have now

A freeform table is a question written in two directions: rows name what is being separated, columns say how it is measured, and every cell is both answered together. A breakdown does not widen that question, it nests a new one inside a row, scoped by everything above it. Rows are dynamic unless you pin them. Attribution lives in the column settings and quietly overrides how a variable was ever configured to behave.

The habit that comes out of all of it is small and worth keeping: before reading any number, read the arrangement that produced it. Which dimension is at the top level, what is indented under what, whether the rows are pinned, and what the percentage is a percentage of. Those four things decide the meaning of every figure on the screen, and none of them are visible in the figure itself.

That is also why the charts in this product are less independent than they look. Every visualization is drawn from a table exactly like this one, inherits its arrangement, and repeats whatever that arrangement decided. Visualizations covers what happens when a number becomes a picture, and why the picture is never the analysis.

Where to find it in Adobe Analytics

A freeform table is the default content of any new panel in Analytics > Workspace. It is also the first entry under the Visualizations icon in the left rail.

Right-click a row for Break down and the row actions. The gear beside a column opens Column settings, which is where attribution, number format and percentages live. Row settings sit under the row header itself.
