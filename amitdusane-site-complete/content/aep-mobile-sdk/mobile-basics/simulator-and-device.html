A simulator is the app running on a laptop pretending to be a phone. The pretence is good enough for building and misleading for measuring.

Before you start

This page will not teach you to build mobile apps. It teaches you enough of the mobile world to understand what a developer is saying, to answer questions in a meeting when no developer is there, and to tell a developer exactly what you need and where it has to change. Words like Xcode, Gradle, native, Flutter and WebView are used here on purpose. You will hear them in every meeting.

Two words that are not the same word

The iPhone tool is a simulator. The Android tool is an emulator. The difference is real rather than branding.

SimulatorRuns your app as an ordinary program on the Mac itself, using the Mac’s processor, memory and network. Very fast, and a weaker imitation than the picture of a handset suggests.
EmulatorRuns a complete Android system inside a virtual machine. A real Android is booting in there, so it is closer to a handset, and slower for that reason.

For your work the difference changes little, because both fall short in the same four places. It is worth knowing only so you understand the room: an Android developer will defend their emulator more strongly than an iPhone developer defends the simulator, and both are right about what they have.

What it gets right

A simulator is excellent at logic, and logic is most of what fails early. If a tracking call sits in the wrong place, fires twice, or carries the wrong value, a simulator shows you immediately and cheaply. If a screen name is misspelled, it is misspelled there too. Nothing about the pretence hides that kind of fault, because that kind of fault lives entirely in the code.

It is also fast, free, and available without borrowing hardware from anybody. While tracking is being built it gets corrected several times an hour, and insisting on a real phone would waste real time for no benefit.

So use the simulator freely while the work is being made. This page is about when to stop, not about whether to start.

Excellent for procedure, useless for weather

Pilots train in flight simulators, and airlines take that training very seriously. Procedures, sequences, instrument reading and emergency drills are all rehearsed there. Practising an engine failure on a real aircraft full of passengers would be absurd. For those purposes the simulator is the correct tool and the aircraft is the wrong one.

What no number of simulator hours does is prove that a particular aircraft is safe to fly. The simulator answers whether the pilot knows the procedure. The aircraft answers whether this machine, in this weather, on this runway, actually flies.

Your split runs along the same line. The simulator answers whether the tracking logic is correct. A real phone on a real network answers whether the measurement works, and that is the question your reports are built on.

Four things a simulator will mislead you about

Installs. An install on a simulator is a developer copying a build into place. It is not a person downloading an app from a store, and the two have almost nothing in common underneath. Install attribution depends on the store transaction and the referral information around it, and none of that runs at all.

The network. A simulator uses the laptop’s connection, which is stable, fast and never in a lift or a basement car park. The SDK’s offline queue exists because real connections fail in the middle of things, and a queue that never fills is a queue nobody has tested. You can force a disconnection, but what you get is a clean one. Real networks produce an ugly half-connected state that is harder to handle and far more common.

Permissions and identifiers. Consent and tracking-permission flows can be demonstrated on a simulator and can look complete. The identifiers behind those prompts do not behave as they do on real hardware, and the advertising identifier in particular cannot be trusted there. So a consent flow can be demonstrated convincingly without ever having been genuinely tested. That is the most persuasive kind of false confidence available to a project.

Everything physical. Real phones are older, slower and fuller than a developer’s laptop. They run out of storage. They lose focus to phone calls and get put to sleep by the operating system to save power. They are held by people who close the app halfway through a checkout. Lifecycle metrics measure exactly those conditions, so a simulator cannot produce an honest one.

The left column is behaviour and the right column is measurement. A demonstration that only exercises the left column is not a validation, however convincing it looks.

Where the line sits

Use a simulator while you are checking behaviour. Move to a real phone the moment you are checking numbers. Everything else on this page explains why the line falls there.

Building tracking, correcting a screen name, confirming an event fires in the right order: simulator, all day, without apology.

Validating install attribution, lifecycle metrics, offline behaviour or consent: a real phone, on a real connection, running a release build, which is what Validate in Assurance asks for. Anything that will be quoted to the business belongs in that second list.

This pairs with the build rule in Debug builds and release builds. A debug build on a real phone is only half the requirement. A release build on a simulator is the other half missing.

Where this goes wrong

An implementation is demonstrated on a simulator, approved in the meeting, and released. Install numbers arrive looking impossible, and the offline queue has never run at all. Nobody misrepresented anything, and every event shown in that meeting was real. None of the conditions the failing metrics depend on had ever been present on a laptop.

What to ask for

Getting a real phone into the loop is usually an organisational problem rather than a technical one. Somebody has to own a spare handset, or add you to a distribution list. Both are easy in week one and awkward in the week before launch.

Three requests worth making early
All three are easier to arrange in week one than in the week before launch.
1
Ask Was this shown on a simulator or a real phone?
Why It is not an accusation, and it changes what the demonstration proved.
2
Ask Is there a TestFlight or internal testing track that can reach my own phone?
Why It lets you validate under real conditions yourself, instead of watching somebody else’s screen.
3
Ask Which metrics have been checked on a real phone, over a real network?
Why Installs, lifecycle, offline queueing and consent are the four a simulator cannot answer for.
✓ What you can now do

You can tell a simulator from an emulator, and know why neither one is a phone. You can use one without guilt while tracking is being built, and stop the moment numbers are being judged. You can name the four things it cannot tell you: installs, real network behaviour, permissions and identifiers, and everything physical about a phone somebody actually owns. And you can ask for a release build on your own phone early enough that getting one is a scheduling question rather than a favour.
