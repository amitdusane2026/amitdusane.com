Nobody writes this down, and then everybody discovers it under pressure, which is the worst possible moment to agree a boundary.

Before you start

This page will not teach you to build mobile apps. It teaches you enough of the mobile world to understand what a developer is saying, to answer questions in a meeting when no developer is there, and to tell a developer exactly what you need and where it has to change. Words like Xcode, Gradle, native, Flutter and WebView are used here on purpose. You will hear them in every meeting.

The line is cleaner than it looks

The boundary is not blurry. It is close to absolute, and it fits in two sentences worth saying out loud in the first week.

Everything on Adobe’s side is yours. The schema, the dataset, the datastream, the tag property, the extensions, the consent default and every environment mapping. You create, change and correct all of it in a browser, without asking anybody, and without a release.

Everything inside the app is theirs. Adding the library, starting the SDK, and placing tracking calls where the app knows something happened. You cannot do any of it, and you should not want to. It goes through their review, their tests and their release, like every other line in the app.

The architect and the builder

An architect does not lay bricks and is not expected to. A builder does not decide where the kitchen goes. Neither is a limitation on the other, and neither role is senior.

They fail in different ways. An architect fails by specifying something unbuildable or unclear. A builder fails by building something other than what was drawn.

What matters is the third thing, which is the drawing. Most disputes on a building site are not about skill on either side. They are about what the drawing did not say, and the assumption each party made instead, silently and reasonably. Your specification is the drawing, and it fails the same way: not because it was wrong, but because it was silent exactly where it needed to be specific.

The seam, and its three items

The seam is small. It has exactly three items, and every one of them is yours to define and theirs to execute. Because they belong to both sides, they are the three things that never get written down.

1. The Environment File ID. One string, given to the developer in Get the SDK into the app, telling the SDK which Adobe property it belongs to. Development and production values differ, and which build gets which is part of the handover. Handing over the wrong one is the quietest failure available: everything works, and the data lands somewhere nobody is watching.

2. The specification of what to track. Each event named, with its fields, the values they carry, and the type of each. Not a description of a goal. A list somebody can build from without asking a follow-up question. If a developer has to ask what a field should contain, the specification was not finished when it was sent.

3. The firing points. Where in the app each event belongs, in the app’s own terms. “When the confirmation screen appears” is a firing point. “On purchase” is a description of a business moment, and it is the sentence that produces the wrong implementation. The developer will pick a moment. It will be a reasonable moment, and it will not necessarily be yours.

Where this goes wrong

A ticket says “track add to cart”. The developer fires it when the button is tapped, which is the obvious reading. You meant when the item is actually in the basket, after the stock check that sometimes fails. The numbers run higher than the order system all year, nobody can find a fault, and both parties can point at the ticket and be right.

When data is missing, which side is it on

The instinct when an event does not arrive is to raise it with the app team. That instinct is usually wrong.

An event can stop in five places. It might not be firing in the app at all, which is the only one on their side. Or it is firing and the datastream is not forwarding it. Or the schema rejects it, because the field does not exist or the type disagrees. Or it is mapped to the wrong dataset. Or consent is withholding it.

Four of those five are yours, and all four are checkable in a browser in a few minutes. So the order of investigation is your side first, entirely, before anything is raised. Assurance will show you whether the event left the phone, which settles the only question that needs them at all.

This is not only courtesy. An analytics person who arrives with “the event is leaving the phone correctly, it is being rejected by the schema, and here is the field” is a colleague. One who arrives with “tracking is broken” three times in a month, and is wrong twice, spends credibility that is needed later.

Owning the specification completely

All of which reduces to one position worth taking early and holding: own the specification completely and the code not at all.

That is not a modest claim, because the specification is the hard part. It needs the business question, the data model, the firing points, and what the app is actually able to tell you. A developer cannot write it and should not have to.

The standard to hold yourself to is one test. A tracking request that names the event, the fields, the values and the firing point is finished, because a developer can build it without asking you anything. Anything less is not a request. It is the start of a conversation, and the conversation is where implementations go wrong.

Three things to agree in week one
All three are conversations, and all three are much harder in week ten.
1
Agree The line out loud: Adobe side yours, app internals theirs, and the three seam items written down.
Why It costs ten minutes now and prevents the two-weeks-before-launch version of the same conversation.
2
Write Every event with its fields, values and firing point, in the app’s own words.
Why A firing point stated as a business moment is the most common cause of a number that never reconciles.
3
Check Your four before raising their one, and bring the Assurance evidence when you do.
Why Four of the five places an event can stop are on your side, which is the opposite of most people’s first instinct.
✓ What you can now do

You can state the boundary in two sentences, and name the three seam items that belong to neither side alone. You can write a tracking request a developer can build without asking a follow-up question, with the firing point in the app’s own words. You investigate your four possible causes before raising the one that is theirs. And you can hold the line that the specification is entirely yours, which is a larger claim than it sounds.
