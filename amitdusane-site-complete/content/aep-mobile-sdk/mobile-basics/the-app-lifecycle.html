A web page has two moments that matter: it loads, and it goes away. An app has neither of those. The states it moves through instead are the reason app numbers behave the way they do.

Before you start

This page will not teach you to build mobile apps. It teaches you enough of the mobile world to understand what a developer is saying, to answer questions in a meeting when no developer is there, and to tell a developer exactly what you need and where it has to change. Words like Xcode, Gradle, native, Flutter and WebView are used here on purpose. You will hear them in every meeting.

A page loads and unloads. An app does neither.

A web page has a clean life. It is requested, it loads, the reader does things, then they leave and it is destroyed. Nothing survives. The next page is a fresh load.

Analytics has hooked onto both ends of that for twenty years. Load is a page view, and leaving is the end of one.

An app is opened once and then stays. It stays in memory while somebody uses it. It stays in memory when they leave it. It is still there, holding everything it had, when they come back forty minutes later. There is no reload and no fresh start.

So the SDK hangs on different moments instead. Those moments are the lifecycle, and there are four states worth knowing by name.

The four states

Not runningInstalled, not in memory. Opening it now is a cold start: everything is built from nothing. Anything the app wrote to storage is still waiting.
ForegroundOn screen, being used. The only state in which anybody is looking at anything, and the only state that counts towards session length.
BackgroundOff screen, still in memory. Somebody took a call or locked the phone. Coming back is a warm start and is nearly instant. This state is what ends your sessions.
TerminatedGone from memory. Sometimes a person swiped it away. Far more often the operating system removed it to free memory. Nobody is told.

The hotel room you keep the key to

Think of the app as a hotel room with your things in it. While you are in the room, you are in the foreground. Step out for dinner and you are in the background. The room is untouched, your things are where you left them, and walking back in costs nothing. You did not check out.

At some point the room is reclaimed, and this is the part with no web equivalent. The hotel decides, not you. It happens while you are out, based on who needs the space more. Nobody phones to tell you. You find out the next time you go back, when you have to check in again and the room is empty.

That is exactly how termination works. The operating system takes memory back from apps that are not on screen whenever something in front needs it. The app is not asked and the person is not told.

Why backgrounding is the state that matters

The SDK ends a session when the app leaves the foreground, and starts a new one when it comes back. A session is foreground time and nothing else. Time spent in the background is not shortening somebody’s session. It is ending it.

Now think how often an app leaves the foreground. A message arrives and is read. A call comes in. The phone locks in a pocket. Somebody checks a train time and comes back. Each of those ends a session, and each return starts another. One twenty-minute shopping trip can honestly be four sessions.

On the web the equivalent boundary is thirty minutes of inactivity, which is a timer rather than an event. A reader who leaves a tab open and returns after ten minutes is still in the same visit. In an app they are not.

That single difference produces most of the gap between app session counts and web visit counts, and no configuration on either side will close it.

The termination you will never see

You cannot reliably measure app closes. When the operating system terminates a backgrounded app, it does not run your code first. There is no final event, no chance to send anything, and no notification afterwards. The app stops existing, and the SDK was not told either.

This is why session length is measured from the foreground events rather than from anything at the end. The close you can see is the app leaving the foreground. What happens after that is invisible by design, on both platforms.

So anybody asking how many people closed the app deliberately is asking for a number that does not exist.

Where this goes wrong

A tracking plan asks for an app-close event. A developer builds one against the last hook the platform offers. It fires when somebody swipes the app away and stays silent for every operating-system termination, which is most of them. The number that arrives looks like a real count of people leaving. It is a partial count of one particular way of leaving.

What this does to your numbers

Three effects follow, and all three arrive without anybody writing a line of tracking code.

Sessions are shorter and far more numerous than web visits. That is correct rather than inflated. Session length measures foreground attention, which is a stricter and more honest measure than a web visit, and it will always look smaller beside one.

Launches are not installs. A launch happens every time the app comes to the foreground, including after a two-second glance at a notification. Installs are the first launch after installation, and upgrades the first after a version change. All of it arrives automatically from two events, switched on in Turn on lifecycle.

Any chart putting app sessions beside web visits will mislead whoever reads it, permanently, whatever the tracking does. The two measure different things by design. A screen is not a page view covers the other half of that mismatch.

What to ask, and what to refuse

Most of the work here is setting expectations before anybody builds a report, rather than after somebody queries one. None of it is configuration.

Three things to settle early
All three are conversations rather than tickets.
1
Say A session ends when the app is backgrounded, so app sessions are shorter and more numerous than web visits.
Why Said at the start it is a design fact. Said after a stakeholder sees the number it sounds like an excuse.
2
Refuse A reliable app close, or a “time until they quit” metric.
Why The operating system terminates apps without running their code, so any number built for this counts one minority case and silently misses the rest.
3
Ask Is automatic lifecycle on, or is the app calling lifecycle start and pause by itself?
Why Modern startup switches it on for you. Older apps do it manually, and that is where launches and sessions go missing.
✓ What you can now do

You can name the four states an app moves through and say which one your engagement numbers measure. You can explain why backgrounding ends a session, and why that alone makes app sessions shorter and more numerous than web visits. You can tell a stakeholder, before they ask, that a dependable app-close metric does not exist and why. And you can recognise that a chart comparing app sessions to web visits is misleading whatever the tracking underneath it does.
