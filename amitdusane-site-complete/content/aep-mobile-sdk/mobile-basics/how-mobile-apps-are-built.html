Somebody chose how this app would be built, probably years ago, probably for reasons that had nothing to do with analytics. You inherit that choice, and almost everything about your project follows from it.

There is no such thing as a mobile app in the singular. There are four broadly different ways to make one. All four produce something that looks identical on a phone. Underneath they are as different as a brick house is from a timber one. Somebody commissioned this app and made the choice years ago. Nobody wrote it down anywhere you can see. Nobody will think to tell you either.

Yet the choice decides which developer you work with, what language your tracking is written in, and how quickly a change can reach you. In one case it decides whether this is even the right guide. So the first useful question in a mobile measurement project is not about measurement at all. It is what kind of app is this, and it belongs in your first conversation.

The book that has to be published in two countries

The reason there are four approaches is that there are two phones. Apple's iPhone runs iOS. Google's ecosystem runs Android. The two have nothing in common underneath: different programming languages, different tools, different rules about what an app may do. A company that wants to reach everybody has to reach both. Each of the four approaches is a different answer to that one problem.

Think of a publisher with a book that has to come out in two countries at once, in two languages. There are a few ways to do that. Each of them trades cost against quality, and you will recognise the trade as soon as you see it.

Native: write it twice, properly

The first approach is to write the app twice. One team builds the iOS version in Apple's language, and another builds the Android version in Google's. The two are genuinely separate programs. They happen to look the same and do the same things, and that is where the resemblance stops. This is what native means. It remains what most large, long-lived, heavily used apps are built as.

In the publishing analogy this is hiring two authors. Each writes for their own country, in their own language, free to use the idioms their readers expect. The result reads perfectly naturally in both places. It also costs about twice as much, and two teams have to maintain it forever.

What native buys is everything the platform can do, the moment the platform can do it. Apple ships a new capability in September. A native iOS app can use it in September. Performance is as good as the device allows. Every Adobe SDK feature works on day one, because the SDK was written for this case first. What native costs, apart from money, is that every request you make lands twice.

Ask for one tracking event and two developers implement it. They work in two languages, on two schedules, from one ticket they each read slightly differently. The two implementations can therefore disagree. That disagreement is quiet, and it is common. An event fires at a slightly different moment on each platform, and the two numbers never reconcile.

Cross-platform: write it once, translate it well

The second approach is to write the app once, in a single language. A framework then produces both versions from that one codebase. React Native and Flutter are the two you will meet. Between them they account for a large and growing share of apps. This is the publisher writing one manuscript and running it through a very good translation engine. One author, one manuscript, two editions that read almost natively. It is enormously cheaper. For most kinds of book it is good enough that readers never notice.

The word doing the work there is almost. A cross-platform framework covers the common ground extremely well. It reaches the edges more slowly. When a platform ships something new, the framework has to catch up before an app can use it, and that lag is measured in months rather than days. Occasionally a joke does not survive translation. The developer then writes a small piece of genuinely native code and bolts it on.

For your purposes the important fact is a reassuring one. Adobe ships plugins for both frameworks, those plugins wrap the same native SDK, and everything on the Adobe side behaves identically. One codebase also means a tracking request lands once rather than twice, which removes an entire class of iOS-and-Android disagreement before it can happen.

Hybrid: put a web page inside an app

The third approach is to build the screens as web pages and wrap them in a thin app that displays them. The container is a real app you download from a store. Much of what you see inside it is a website, rendering in a component called a WebView. Apache Cordova and Ionic are the names you will hear. A great many apps use this for part of themselves rather than all of themselves.

The publisher here has printed the same pages for both countries and bound them in a locally produced cover. It is the cheapest of the four by a wide margin. It ships fastest. Readers can usually tell.

This one matters to you more than the others. It is the case where the answer to your question may not be the Mobile SDK at all. If a screen is a web page, it can carry the Web SDK, exactly as your website does. If the shell around it is a real app, that shell can carry the Mobile SDK.

An app that is substantially WebView may need one, the other, or a carefully agreed combination. Getting that decision wrong is expensive in a way the other three are not, because it surfaces late and it gets retrofitted under pressure.

The question worth asking on day one

If anybody says the app is Cordova, Ionic, or "mostly web views", stop and work out where the screens actually live before agreeing to any implementation approach. That single question can change the entire shape of the project, and it is far cheaper to ask now than to discover in testing.

Game engines, and the apps nobody expects

The fourth approach is a game engine, most often Unity. Games are built this way almost universally. So are a surprising number of things that are not games. Retail experiences with three-dimensional product views are built in Unity. So are training simulators and some interactive marketing apps. These are a different world again, with their own tooling and their own conventions, and Adobe's support for them is thinner. If you find yourself on one, expect the implementation to need more custom work. Expect fewer people to have done it before you, inside your company and outside it.

How to find out which one you have

You do not need to read any code to answer this. Ask, and the answer arrives in one sentence. But ask precisely. "It is an app" is not an answer, and people offer it in good faith, because to them the question sounded like it had already been settled. The three questions below are the precise version, and asking them in the first conversation costs you nothing at all.

Three questions, in the first conversation
Any developer can answer all three in under a minute.
1
Ask Is there one codebase or two? Two means native. One means cross-platform, hybrid or a game engine.
2
Ask What is it written in? Swift or Kotlin means native. JavaScript or TypeScript means React Native or hybrid. Dart means Flutter. C# usually means Unity.
3
Ask How much of the screen is a web view? If the answer is anything other than "none", find out exactly which screens before going further.

What actually changes for you

Less than you might fear, and the part that does change is worth knowing precisely. Everything on the Adobe side is identical in all four cases. The schema does not change. Neither does the datastream, the tag property, the extensions, consent, identity or Assurance. None of them knows how the app was built, and none of them cares. They sit on Adobe's servers and receive events that look the same whatever produced them.

What differs is on the app side, and it comes down to three things. Which language the tracking code is written in. How the SDK gets added to the project. And how many times each of your requests has to be implemented.

NativeSwift and Kotlin. Two implementations of every request, on two schedules. Full SDK support, immediately.
React NativeJavaScript or TypeScript. One implementation. A plugin wraps the native SDK, and occasionally trails it.
FlutterDart. One implementation. Same wrapper arrangement as React Native.
Hybrid or WebViewWeb technologies inside a native shell. May need the Web SDK instead, or as well. Decide this before anything else.
Game engineUsually C#. Thinner Adobe support and more custom work. Rare, and worth flagging early if you meet one.
✓ What you can now do

Sit in a kick-off meeting and ask three short questions. The answers tell you whether this is two codebases or one, and which language your tracking will be written in. They also tell you whether the WebView question has to be settled before anything else starts. That is genuinely most of what this decision costs you.
