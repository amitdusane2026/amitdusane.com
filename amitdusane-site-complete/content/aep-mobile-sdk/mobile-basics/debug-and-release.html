A developer saying “it works on mine” is usually telling the truth about a build that nobody else will ever run.

Validation goes well. A developer shares their screen. The events appear in Assurance in the right order, carrying the right values. Everybody in the meeting agrees the implementation is finished. Three weeks after release the numbers are wrong, and wrong in a way that matches nothing anybody saw in testing. The obvious explanation is that something changed between the test and the release. That is where people look first, and there is nothing there to find.

Nothing was faked and nobody was careless. The session was real, the events were real, and the code did not change. They came from a different app than the one your customers installed, compiled from exactly the same source. Understanding how that is possible is the difference between validation that means something and validation that merely looks like it did.

One source, two apps

Every project produces at least two kinds of build from one body of code. A debug build is what a developer runs while working. A release build is what goes to a store. They come from identical source files and they are not identical programs. The instructions given to the compiler are different in each case.

A debug build keeps verbose logging, so a developer can watch what the app is doing line by line. It skips most optimisation, which makes it compile quickly and run slowly. It frequently points at test services rather than production ones. Every one of those choices exists to make the app easier to inspect while it is being worked on. All of them are correct choices for that purpose.

A release build inverts each of them. Logging is stripped out entirely. The compiler optimises aggressively, rearranging work and discarding anything it can prove is unnecessary. The build is signed for distribution and pointed at production services. It is made to be shipped rather than inspected. It is also the only one of the two that ever reaches a customer, or produces a number your business acts on.

The dress rehearsal

The comparison worth holding is a dress rehearsal against opening night. A rehearsal is the same play, the same cast, and the same script. It also has the house lights half up and a prompter audible from the wings. A director can stop everything and take a scene again from the top. Everybody in the building knows the conditions are generous, and that is the point of holding one.

Opening night removes all of it at once. The play has not changed and the conditions carrying it have. Anything the prompter was quietly covering is now exposed, in front of the only audience whose opinion counts. There is no mechanism for stopping. A company that rehearsed well can still be caught by something that the rehearsal conditions were hiding rather than testing.

That is precisely the relationship between the two builds. The logging a developer reads is the prompter. The relaxed timing of an unoptimised build is the pause while somebody finds their place. Both vanish on the night. Neither announces in advance what it was covering, which is why the failure always looks like it came from nowhere.

Three ways the difference bites

Most of the time the two builds behave identically, and that is exactly what makes the differences dangerous. A difference that appeared every time would have been found and fixed years ago. These appear occasionally, which is frequent enough to reach production and rare enough to survive testing.

Optimisation changes timing. A release build runs faster and reorders work that a debug build performed in a leisurely sequence. Code can hold a race condition, where two things assume an order that nobody actually guaranteed. That code behaves correctly for years in debug, and fails in release. The version that costs you events is initialisation racing the first screen. An event fired before the SDK is ready is not queued and not retried. It is simply gone.

Stripped logging removes the evidence. The log lines a developer relied on to prove the implementation worked are not present in a release build. So the build you most need to understand is the one that tells you least about itself. Unless validation was designed around that fact from the beginning, the natural response is to fall back to a debug build. That is how the whole problem repeats.

The two builds usually report to different places. This is the one that produces wrong numbers rather than missing ones. It is common enough to deserve its own section.

Where the test traffic goes

The SDK is told which Adobe property it belongs to by an Environment File ID. That value very often differs between debug and release. That is correct and deliberate rather than a mistake. Development traffic should land in a development environment, and production traffic in production. Separating them is exactly what the two configurations are for.

It also means the build a developer validates on and the build the business reports from point at two different destinations. Everything can look perfect in the development environment for weeks. The production configuration meanwhile carries a value nobody has checked. Checking it means building the release configuration and looking somewhere else. The implementation was never wrong. It was never tested where it mattered.

The failure runs in the other direction too, and that version is more embarrassing. A build configured to report to production gets handed round the office for a fortnight of testing. It quietly fills the report the business is watching with staff traffic. Nothing errors and no alert fires. The numbers simply include forty people who were never customers, and who all behave like unusually thorough shoppers.

On iOS the value is usually decided by the build configuration. That is one of the three words covered in iOS: Swift, Xcode, and what your developer is looking at. So asking which configuration produced a build is the same question as asking where its data went. It is phrased in the language the person answering already uses.

Where this goes wrong

An implementation is signed off from a debug build, on a developer's machine, pointed at the development environment. Every event is correct. The release build carries a placeholder Environment File ID that was never updated, and reports nowhere at all. The gap is not discovered until somebody opens the production report after launch, by which time the fix needs another release and another store review.

The build worth signing off on

The rule that follows from all of this is short enough to hold in one line. Final validation happens on a release build, and on nothing else. Not a debug build with logging turned down. Not a release-signed debug build either, which sounds close enough to count and is not. An actual release build, produced by the release configuration, ideally the exact artefact intended for the store.

Both platforms provide a way to get one into your hands before the public has it. Apple has TestFlight, and Google Play has internal and closed testing tracks. Both distribute a genuine release build to a named list of people, which is the whole reason they exist. That build is the last version identical to what a customer will install. Being identical is what makes it worth validating against.

Validating there costs something, and it is worth saying plainly rather than discovering it mid-project. Logging is gone, so you are reading Assurance instead of a developer's console. The loop is slower, because each attempt needs a build rather than a rebuild on somebody's machine. That is the price of testing the artefact that ships, instead of a rehearsal of it. It is worth paying once at the end rather than never.

What to ask

Three questions settle every case on this page. All three are about the build rather than about the code, which is the shift What a build actually is argues for. None of them requires a developer to prepare anything.

Before signing anything off
Ask these of the specific build in front of you, every time.
1
Ask Is this a debug or a release build, and was it produced by the release configuration?
Why A yes to the first half and a no to the second is the most common way a sign-off comes to mean nothing.
2
Ask Which Environment File ID does this build carry?
Why It tells you where the data you are about to look at actually went, which is not always where you are looking for it.
3
Ask Is this build available through TestFlight or an internal testing track?
Why It puts a genuine release build on your own phone, which is where validation should finish rather than start.
✓ What you can now do

Explain why one body of source code produces two different apps, and why the friendlier one is not the one that matters. Name the three ways they diverge: timing, evidence and destination. Ask which Environment File ID a build carries, and understand that the answer decides where the numbers you are about to read came from. And refuse, politely, to sign anything off from a debug build.
