Diagram Specification

Every diagram on the learning world is drawn to this. It exists because the corpus currently contains two visible generations — 76 diagrams built as CSS boxes and 65 drawn as SVG — and because without a written standard the next seven modules would add a third.

The design system here is this site’s own, and it is already settled. The type scale, the spacing scale, the colour tokens, the diagram-box component and the 700px figure width are all decided and in use across 116 sections. This document does not import a look from anywhere; it says how to draw inside that system. Where the migration guide is mentioned it is as evidence that a standard of figure is achievable, never as a template to match — it is a different world with a different accent, a different shell and a different type scale, and nothing about it should be fitted onto this one.

00

The reader's test, which outranks everything below

Amit's criteria, and they are the acceptance test. A diagram that fails any of these is not finished, however well it follows the mechanics in the rest of this document.

  1. Does it communicate the story? Not the components — the story. If the caption and the drawing say the same thing, the drawing is redundant.
  2. Does it land in two seconds? A reader should understand the shape of the idea before reading a single label. If they have to study it, it has failed.
  3. Does it render well on a phone and on a desktop? Both. Not one.
  4. Is it attractive? Would you be happy for it to be the first thing someone sees on the site.
  5. Does it teach as immediately as the migration guide’s diagrams do? Not look like them — that guide is a different world with a different palette and a different shell. The bar is the effect: a reader gets the mental model before they get the detail. It is worth knowing that bar has already been cleared on this site, so it is not a hypothetical.
The two-second test is the one that fails most often. A diagram with nine boxes and eleven arrows may be accurate and still fail, because the reader cannot see the shape before they start reading. Accuracy is not the constraint. Comprehension is.
01

The four rules that make the difference

These are what separate a drawing that teaches from a labelled box arrangement. They are about how to think about a figure. Everything after them — size, colour, spacing, weight — comes from this site’s own design system and is stated in its own tokens.

RULE 1

Draw the metaphor. Do not label it.

The strongest diagram on the whole site draws a house — roof, walls, interior walls, a padlock on one room — to explain an XDM schema. It does not draw a box with the word "blueprint" in it. The reader understands rooms and doors before they have read anything, and the technical structure beside it inherits that understanding.

If the section carries an extended analogy in its prose, the diagram is where that analogy becomes visible. If it does not, ask whether it needs one.

RULE 2

Two panels: the idea, then the reality.

Metaphor on the left, the actual thing on the right, joined by a single dashed connector. Or before and after. Or wrong and right. The eye travels left to right once, and the arrow tells it that the two sides are the same thing said twice.

It is the most reliable way to hit the two-second test, and it is a general information-design pattern rather than a house style. It is not the only permitted shape: the real rule is one movement for the eye. A figure the eye has to traverse twice has two ideas in it and should be two figures.

RULE 3

Wide and short, never tall.

Every canvas is viewBox="0 0 700 H" with H between 180 and 340. A tall diagram is a diagram that has to be scrolled past rather than seen, and on a phone it is the difference between a glance and a chore.

If the content will not fit in 340, it is two diagrams, or it is a table.

RULE 4

The title states the takeaway.

The diagram-title is not a label. "Schema structure" is a label. "The schema decides where every value lives, like a house plan — and once Profile is on, the structure locks" is a takeaway. A reader who reads only the title and glances at the drawing should still leave with the point.

02

Whether to draw at all

The current count is a starting point, not a target. Granted by Amit, 19 August 2026: a cycle may add a figure where the subject deserves one and remove a figure that is not earning its place. The instruction was to take a wise call in both directions — free to deviate, not free to run away with it.

Why this has to be written down

Every quality mechanism on this site can only add. The rulebook checklist finds things absent or malformed. The QA gate finds things missing. A comparison against a generated reference finds things not covered. None of them can say cut this. Left alone, that drifts the site toward a reference document one defensible addition at a time.

The redraw programme is the moment that bias is most dangerous, because redrawing invites elaboration: it is far easier to make a figure bigger than to decide it should not exist. So the authority to delete is stated here as plainly as the standard to draw to.

Add a figure when

  • The reader has to hold three or more things in their head at once to follow the paragraph. That is what a figure is for.
  • The idea is spatial or relational — what sits inside what, what flows where, what happens before what. Prose is bad at these and a drawing is good at them.
  • The section carries an extended analogy in its prose and the analogy is never drawn. That is the strongest addition available, and the site has several sections in that position.
  • Two things are being compared and confused in practice. A side-by-side settles it faster than any paragraph.

Remove a figure when

  • It restates the sentence above it. If the prose already carried the point, the figure is decoration.
  • It is really a table or a list wearing boxes. Three labelled rectangles in a row is a list. Set it as one.
  • It is redundant with a neighbouring figure in the same section, which happens most often where two sections were written weeks apart.
  • It exists to break up the page rather than to explain something.
The deletion test. Cover the figure and read the section. If nothing is lost, it was never carrying anything, and removing it makes the ones that remain count for more.
The guardrail. A module's diagram count should not swing wildly. If a cycle wants to double a module's figures, the module probably has a content problem the figures are being asked to solve.

Record every addition and removal in the cycle's commit, with the reason. A count that moves silently is indistinguishable from drift, which is the thing this section exists to prevent.

03

Type

Since the figure now holds --diagram-min (700px) at every width, authored px is rendered px. A font-size="12" is 12px on the reader's screen. That was not true before August and it is why the old sizes cannot simply be copied.

SizeWeightJob
16700The one thing the diagram is about. At most one per diagram, often none.
14700Panel headings — the name of each side of a two-panel figure.
12.5600Node labels. The workhorse; most text is this.
11400Annotations, leaf labels, units, asides. The floor.
Nothing below 11. The corpus currently has 141 places at 8 to 10.5px, 16 of them under the legibility floor for any handset. They are known and they are not a precedent.

Sentence case, not capitals

Panel headings are sentence case bold, matching the label voice used by every box on the page. Capitals plus tracking is half of a recognisable generated-page signature, and this site has already rejected it once. A figure is part of the page, not a separate object with its own typography.

Never mono inside a diagram, except where the text is code or a field path.

Colour roles

All colour comes from tokens, in a style attribute with a hex fallback. A presentation attribute like fill="var(--accent)" does not resolve reliably.

TokenRole in a diagram
--dia-a … --dia-eThe diagram palette. Five hues — crimson, blue, green, amber, violet — each with a -soft tint and a -ink. Use them to encode categories, stages or levels. The saturated value is for strokes, arrows and solid shapes that carry no text; the -soft plus -ink pair is for anything with a label on it.
--accentThe subject. What the diagram is about, and nothing else.
--accent-lightFill behind the subject.
--card / --bg3Neutral node fills.
--borderNeutral node strokes and structural lines.
--text1 / --text2Node labels.
--text3Annotations and asides.
--danger-ink / --warning-ink / --success-ink / --info-inkA judgement: this is wrong, risky, correct, or worth knowing. The -ink forms, never the bare semantic — the bare ones cannot carry text.
--on-accentAny text sitting on an accent or -ink fill. Never a hardcoded #ffffff.
Colour has to do a job. Encode something real — a category, a stage, a level, a verdict — and then a reader can learn it and carry it to the next figure. There is no cap on how many hues a figure uses and no virtue in using one.
Colour must not decorate. If two boxes are different colours for no reason a reader could state, they should be the same colour. That is the only restriction, and it is about meaning rather than about count.

Bright is allowed. The constraint is on text, not on colour

Amit's ruling, 19 August 2026: no rule to stick to one colour, no rule to use many, and no restriction to darker colours. Vivid and fluorescent are available.

What keeps that safe is a split, and it is the whole trick: a shape that carries text takes the soft tint as its fill, the saturated line as its stroke, and the ink as its text. A shape that carries no text — a chip, a dot, a bar, an arrowhead — can be the saturated colour at full strength. The vivid values never sit behind letters, so they never have to be dimmed to stay readable.

The palette is five hues, fixed in world-learning.css, so twenty-one modules stay recognisable as one system rather than each picking its own. Every ink is measured against its own tint: 5.46:1 or better in light, 7.99:1 or better in dark.

The id collision trap

Marker and gradient ids are global to the page. Two diagrams in one section both defining #arrow means the second silently uses the first's. Suffix every id with the section number: as3, ms3, os3.

06

The pass, per diagram

Run this before calling any diagram done, whether it was redrawn or inherited.

  1. Does it earn its place at all? Cover it and read the section. If nothing is lost, remove it rather than redraw it — see 02.
  2. Cover the labels. Is the shape still meaningful? If not, it is a box arrangement, not a diagram.
  3. Read only the title. Does the point survive?
  4. Count drawn elements. 20–45.
  5. Smallest authored font-size is 11 or above.
  6. Canvas is 700 wide and no taller than 340.
  7. No hardcoded colour anywhere; every fill and stroke is a token with a fallback.
  8. Every colour used encodes something a reader could name. Vivid is fine; text never sits on a saturated fill.
  9. role="img" and a full-sentence aria-label present.
  10. Marker ids are section-suffixed.
  11. Rendered check at 390px and 1280px, in both themes.
